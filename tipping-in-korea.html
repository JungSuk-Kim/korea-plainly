Do You Tip in Korea? What Visitors Should Know

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 16 October 2026

Tipping is not a normal part of everyday service in Korea. Learn what to expect at restaurants, in taxis, at hotels, and on tours—and what to do if a tip is requested.

In most situations, you do not need to tip in Korea. Tipping is not a standard part of everyday service, and workers generally do not expect an extra payment on top of the bill. Visitors can pay the amount shown and leave without worrying that they have been rude.

Practices can vary at some upscale hotels, restaurants, or tourist services, so check the bill or booking details if something is unclear. But there is no need to carry small bills for routine tips or to add a percentage automatically.

Restaurants and cafes

At ordinary restaurants, the price on the menu is what you should expect to pay. You do not need to leave extra money on the table after a meal. This applies whether you order at a counter or are served at your table.

Cafes also do not generally expect tips. If you see a tip jar, adding something is optional; it is not a signal that you must pay extra. A digital payment screen may show a prompt in some places, especially businesses used to international customers. If you do not want to tip, choose the option for no tip or continue with the standard payment amount.

Check your receipt if you are unsure what has been charged. Some businesses may list a service charge separately, particularly in more formal settings. If a charge is already included, you do not need to add another tip. Do not assume that every restaurant adds one, either: the bill and menu are the best guide.

Taxis and other rides

Taxi drivers do not normally expect a tip. Pay the fare shown on the meter or in the app. You may round up for convenience if you want, but it is not required, and there is no standard percentage to add.

If you pay in cash, you can ask for the correct change. If you pay by card or through a ride app, follow the payment total shown. A driver who helps with luggage or gives useful directions is usually thanked with a simple “thank you,” rather than a cash tip.

Hotels

At most hotels, you are not expected to tip housekeeping staff, reception staff, or people who help with your bags. If you want to recognize especially helpful service, a sincere thank-you is enough. A tip left in a room may not be understood as intended, so do not feel that you need to leave cash each day.

Some high-end hotels may have their own service-charge policies or procedures for gratuities. Check the hotel’s information or ask staff if you are unsure. If a service charge appears on your bill, check what it covers before adding anything extra.

Food delivery, salons, and everyday services

For food delivery, pay the amount shown in the app or by the business. Delivery fees and other listed charges are separate from a tip, but an extra payment is not normally expected. If an app offers an optional tip, you can choose whether to use it; you do not have to add one to receive normal service.

At hair salons and other personal-care businesses, customers generally pay the listed price without tipping. The same basic approach works for many everyday services: check the stated price, pay it, and do not assume that a tip is part of the transaction.

Tours and special services

Tour guides and drivers do not automatically expect tips in the way they might in some other countries. For a private tour, a paid excursion, or a service arranged through a travel company, check the booking terms. They may explain whether anything extra is included or invited. A group tour may also have its own instructions, so follow the information from the operator rather than guessing.

If a guide has gone well beyond what you expected and you want to show appreciation, you can offer a tip, but it remains optional. A clear thank-you or a positive review is also a useful way to recognize good service.

What if someone asks for a tip?

A request for a tip may feel unfamiliar, but you can take a moment to check whether it is an optional gratuity, a listed service charge, or simply a payment prompt. Ask what the charge is for if you are uncertain. You can politely decline an optional tip without making a scene.

  • Look at the receipt or payment screen before confirming the total.
  • Ask whether a service charge is already included.
  • Do not add a tip just because a screen displays a suggested amount.
  • Keep any tip you choose to give small and voluntary; there is no standard amount to follow.

The simplest rule

For most everyday purchases in Korea, pay the amount shown and do not add a tip. Tipping is not a test of politeness, and skipping it is unlikely to be seen as disrespectful. When a business clearly explains a service charge or an optional gratuity, use that information to decide. Otherwise, a friendly thank-you is all that is needed.

Bottom line: A little preparation can make your time in Korea much easier. Keep this guide handy and enjoy exploring at your own pace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I tip at restaurants in Korea?

Usually, no. Tipping is not customary in Korean restaurants, and staff generally do not expect extra money after a meal. Pay the amount shown on the bill. If a service charge is listed, you do not need to add another tip. A sincere thank-you is appreciated.

Do I need to tip taxi drivers?

No tip is normally expected for a taxi ride. Pay the fare shown on the meter or app. If you want to show appreciation, you can simply thank the driver; rounding up is optional, not a requirement. Don’t feel obliged to add money because you are visiting.

Should I tip hotel staff, tour guides, or delivery workers?

Tipping is generally not part of everyday service in Korea, so you are not expected to add money for hotel help, guided tours, or food delivery. If someone has gone out of their way, a warm thank-you is appropriate. Follow any clearly stated fees or payment instructions rather than assuming a tip is required.
