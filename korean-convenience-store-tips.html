Korean Convenience Stores: CU vs GS25, What to Eat and How They Work

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 7 October 2026 · Updated 31 October 2026

CU, GS25, 7-Eleven and emart24: what to eat, how to pay and heat your food, and the services visitors use most.

You are never far from a convenience store in Korea. CU, GS25, 7-Eleven and emart24 sit on almost every block in the cities, many open 24 hours, and for visitors they are far more than a place to buy water: they are where you grab a cheap breakfast, top up your transit card, heat up a late-night meal and pick up snacks to take home. Here is how they work, what to eat and which phrases help.

The main chains

All four big chains sell much the same things, so the nearest branch is usually the right one. The differences are in their own-brand snacks, meal boxes and monthly deals.

  • CU (purple and green sign) and GS25 (blue sign) are the two biggest, with well over ten thousand stores each. Both are strong on lunch boxes, triangle rice balls and their own snack lines.
  • 7-Eleven is the brand you may know from home, but the range is Korean, not a copy of stores elsewhere.
  • emart24 (yellow and grey) is smaller in number, run by the E-Mart supermarket group, and often has a decent wine and imported snack shelf.

The branch matters more than the brand. A big store near a station or university may have tables, a ramyeon station and a wide food choice; a small one on a side street may have only drinks and packaged snacks.

What to eat

Dosirak (lunch boxes)

Boxed meals with rice, a main dish and a few sides, such as bulgogi, pork cutlet or spicy chicken, usually around ₩4,000 to ₩7,000 (2026). Heat them in the store's microwave; the heating time is printed on the label.

Samgak gimbap (triangle rice balls)

Triangles of rice and seaweed filled with tuna mayonnaise, spicy pork or kimchi, often under ₩2,000. Open them by pulling the numbered tabs in order: tab 1 down the middle, then 2 and 3 to each side, so the seaweed stays crisp around the rice.

Cup ramyeon

Peel the lid back to the line, add water from the hot-water dispenser to the marked level, wait three or four minutes and eat at the window counter. Stores in the Han River parks in Seoul have machines that cook packet ramyeon in a foil bowl, a favourite picnic ritual.

Gimbap rolls, sandwiches and hot snacks

Full gimbap rolls and sandwiches are good for train journeys. Near the counter, look for steamed buns, hot dogs, fried chicken pieces and fish cakes, depending on the brand and season.

Drinks and treats

Banana-flavoured milk is the classic. Iced coffee comes in pouches that you pour into a cup of ice from the freezer, and Korean ice creams and snacks make easy, cheap gifts to take home.

Save money with 1+1 and 2+1

Shelf signs saying 1+1 mean buy one, get one free; 2+1 means buy two, get a third free. Deals change every month and differ between chains. The free item is usually the same product or one from the same promotion, so bring them all to the counter together.

Paying

Put your things on the counter and the cashier scans them. Foreign Visa and Mastercard cards work in almost every store, and so do transit cards such as T-money. If one card is declined, try another or pay in cash. Bags cost a little extra, so bring your own or ask for one.

Alcohol and cigarettes are sold only to adults (19 and over under Korean rules), and young-looking customers may be asked for ID, so carry your passport or a photo of it.

Eating in the store

  • Pay first, then use the microwave yourself; take off foil and metal lids, and ask the staff if you cannot work it out.
  • Eat at the window counter or at the tables inside or outside the store. It is completely normal, at any hour.
  • Clean up after yourself: pour leftover soup into the drain or bucket provided and sort rubbish where bins are marked.

T-money, ATMs and other services

  • T-money: CU, GS25, 7-Eleven and emart24 sell transit cards and top them up at the counter, usually with cash only, so keep a few ₩10,000 notes. See our T-money guide.
  • ATMs: many stores have one. For a foreign card, look for one marked "Global" or with your card network's logo; see our ATM guide.
  • Toilets: most convenience stores do not have a customer toilet. Subway stations are the reliable option.
  • Labels and allergies: labels are in Korean, and many snacks contain meat, fish or seafood extracts. Use a camera translation app such as Papago, and watch for 매운 (maeun, spicy) and red packaging.

Three phrases worth saving

  • 봉투 주세요 (bongtu juseyo): A bag, please.
  • 이거 데워 주세요 (igeo dewo juseyo): Please heat this.
  • T-money 충전해 주세요 (T-money chungjeonhae juseyo): Please top up my T-money.
Bottom line: pick up your food, pay at the counter, heat it yourself and eat at the window. Keep a little cash for T-money top-ups, and check the 1+1 signs before you choose a drink.

FAQ

Is it OK to eat inside a Korean convenience store?

Yes. Most stores have a counter or tables, and eating there is completely normal, day or night. Just clean up afterwards.

Can I pay with a foreign card at a Korean convenience store?

Almost always. Visa and Mastercard work in nearly every store, but T-money top-ups usually need cash.

Which is better, CU or GS25?

Neither is clearly better: both sell much the same things at similar prices. The nearest, bigger branch is usually the best choice; compare the 1+1 and 2+1 deals if you want a particular snack.

Are Korean convenience stores open 24 hours?

Most in cities are, which makes them a lifesaver after a late arrival, but some branches in office buildings or quiet areas close at night.
