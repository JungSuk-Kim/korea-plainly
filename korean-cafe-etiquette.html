Korean Cafe Etiquette for First-Time Visitors

By Korea Plainly · Published 7 October 2026 · Updated 13 October 2026

How Korean cafes work: ordering at the counter or kiosk, buzzers, one drink per person, staying to work, returning your tray and what to order.

Korea Plainly · 5 min read

Photo: H. Y. Shin 000, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons

Korea has one of the world's biggest cafe cultures. Cities are full of independent coffee shops, hanok cafes, bakery cafes and theme cafes, and locals use them as living rooms, offices and meeting spots. Cafes work a little differently from many countries, so here is what to expect.

Ordering and paying

In almost every Korean cafe you order and pay at the counter first, then wait for your drink. Many cafes, especially chains, use a kiosk: choose your drinks, pick "for here" or "to go", and pay by card. Look for an English button on the screen. If a kiosk doesn't accept your card, ask at the counter.

You will often get a buzzer (a vibrating pager) or a number. When it goes off or your number appears, collect your order from the pick-up counter yourself; staff usually don't bring it to your table.

What to order

  • Iced Americano is Korea's everyday coffee, ordered all year round, even in winter.
  • Lattes and flavoured drinks such as sweet potato, grain (misutgaru) or citron (yuja) tea are popular local options.
  • Bingsu, shaved ice with toppings, is a favourite in summer and big enough to share.
  • Many cafes sell excellent cakes and pastries, and bakery cafes can be destinations in their own right.

Sizes are often listed as regular and large, or tall, grande and venti in chains. Decaf is increasingly common; look for 디카페인 (dikapein).

Staying and sitting

  • One drink per person is the general expectation, especially in smaller cafes.
  • Long stays are normal. Many Koreans study or work in cafes for hours. In busy times, it is polite to buy another drink if you stay a long while, or to free up the table.
  • Saving seats: you will see people leave a laptop or phone on a table to keep it. Theft in cafes is rare, but keep your own valuables with you.
  • Outside food and drink are generally not allowed.
  • Many cafes have several floors or a rooftop, so look upstairs before deciding the cafe is full.

Cups and returning your tray

If you drink in, many cafes serve you in a mug or glass rather than a disposable cup. When you leave, take your tray, cups and plates to the return area, often near the counter. Some cafes have a station where you separate leftover ice and drink, cups, straws and lids. Follow the signs or copy what others do.

Other things to know

  • Restrooms: some cafes share a building restroom that needs a door code; it is often printed on your receipt or posted by the counter.
  • Wi-Fi: most cafes have free Wi-Fi; the password is usually on the receipt or a sign.
  • No-kids zones: a few cafes don't allow young children. It is usually posted at the door.
  • Volume: people talk freely, but loud phone calls are frowned upon in quiet cafes.
  • Tipping: not expected.

Cafes worth seeking out

Korean cafes are an attraction in their own right. Try a hanok cafe such as Cafe Onion Anguk in Seoul, a sea-view cafe on the coast like Waveon Coffee in Busan, or a renowned roastery such as Terarosa in Gangneung, a city famous for its coffee streets.

FAQ

Do Korean cafes have table service?

Rarely. You order at the counter or kiosk and collect your drink when your buzzer goes off.

Can I work on my laptop in a Korean cafe?

Yes, it is very common. Be considerate at busy times and order again if you stay for hours.

Why does everyone drink iced Americano?

It is simply Korea's favourite everyday coffee, light and refreshing, and people order it in every season.
