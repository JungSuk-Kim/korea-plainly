Noraebang Meaning and Etiquette: How Korean Karaoke Rooms Work

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 17 October 2026 · Updated 16 November 2026

Noraebang meaning explained: how Korean karaoke rooms work, coin vs regular rooms, finding English songs, rough prices and etiquette for visitors.

Noraebang (노래방) is the Korean word for karaoke, and the meaning is literal: norae means song and bang means room, so a noraebang is a “song room”. Unlike a karaoke bar in many countries, you never sing in front of strangers. Your group rents a small soundproofed room with a big screen, two microphones, a sofa and disco lights, and the room is yours until the timer runs out. Koreans go after dinner with colleagues, on birthdays, after exams, or simply because it is raining. For a visitor it is one of the easiest pieces of everyday Korean life to join in.

Two kinds of noraebang

You will meet two main types, and they work quite differently.

  • Regular noraebang (노래방). A counter with staff, rooms for anything from two to fifteen people, and charging by the hour per room. As a rough guide in 2026, expect somewhere around 15,000–30,000 won an hour for a small room, more for a large room or on Friday and Saturday nights in busy areas such as Hongdae or Gangnam. Some places charge per person instead, so ask.
  • Coin noraebang (코인노래방, often shortened to “kono”). Rows of tiny booths for one or two people, with no staff at the door. You feed cash or tap a card at the machine in the booth and pay by the song, or buy a block of time. At many places 1,000 won buys a few songs, which makes it the cheapest way to try. Coin booths are perfect for a quick solo session but too cramped for a group.

Both are easy to spot: look for the word 노래방 or 코인노래방 on a bright sign, often on the second floor or in a basement. University areas such as Hongdae and Sinchon are full of them, and most stay open late into the night. Prices and hours change, so check the venue on Naver Map or KakaoMap before you go.

One warning: avoid places signed 단란주점 (danranjujeom) or 유흥주점 (yuheungjujeom). These are hostess-style drinking venues that also have singing rooms, and the bill can be many times what you expected. An ordinary noraebang is a family-friendly business.

Arriving and paying

At a regular noraebang, walk up to the counter, hold up fingers for your group size and say “hanshigan” (한 시간, one hour). Staff will tell you the price, usually take payment up front by card or cash, and point you to a room number. Foreign cards work at most counters, but a little cash is useful at older or smaller places (see cash and cards in Korea).

The timer on the screen counts down in minutes. Many owners quietly add “service” (서비스) time when you are close to the end, especially on quiet nights, so do not be surprised if ten or twenty minutes appear. If you want more, go back to the counter and ask for another thirty minutes or an hour before the timer hits zero.

Finding songs, including English ones

Every room has a thick songbook and a remote control or tablet. Korean machines come from two main makers, TJ and KY (Kumyoung), and each song has a number. Type the number and press the reserve button to queue it. The buttons you need most are:

  • 예약 (yeyak) – reserve, adds the song to the queue
  • 우선예약 (useon yeyak) – priority reserve, puts it next
  • 취소 (chwiso) – cancel, stops the current song
  • 시작 (sijak) – start

The songbook has an English or “POP” section near the back, and the large catalogues include thousands of English, Japanese and Chinese songs, though newer foreign hits can be missing. The quickest trick is to look up song numbers before you go: the TJ and KY song search websites list numbers by title and artist, and many people keep a note on their phone. Newer tablet systems let you search by typing an English title directly. K-pop fans will find almost every title, often with the official video playing behind the lyrics.

Noraebang etiquette

There are few rules, and the mood is generous. Nobody cares whether you can sing; enthusiasm counts for more.

  • Take turns choosing songs and do not queue five in a row while others wait.
  • Join in on choruses, clap, and use the tambourines. Cheering for a bad singer is part of the fun.
  • Pick something everyone knows when the room goes quiet. A classic upbeat K-pop track or a well-known English pop song lifts the mood.
  • Handle the microphones gently; do not swing them or tap them to test.
  • Order drinks and snacks at the counter or with the room phone or button. Ordinary noraebang sell soft drinks, and many sell beer, but the rules on alcohol depend on the licence, so do not bring your own bottles.
  • Leave the room reasonably tidy when you go.

The score that flashes up after each song is random enough that no one takes it seriously, though a 100 always gets a cheer.

Practical tips for first-timers

  • Go after dinner. The classic Korean evening is dinner, then drinks, then noraebang as the “second” or “third round”. Rooms are busiest from about 9pm, especially at weekends.
  • Under-18s. Teenagers are generally allowed in during the day and early evening, but late-night entry for minors is restricted unless they are with a parent or guardian. Families with children should go early.
  • Solo travellers. A coin noraebang is the easy option. Nobody will find it strange to sing alone.
  • Language. Staff rarely need more than your group size and time. A translation app such as Papago covers anything else, and a few useful Korean phrases go a long way.
  • Combine it with a night out. Noraebang cluster in the same streets as bars and restaurants; our guide to Korea’s nightlife districts shows where to find them.
Bottom line: Noraebang means “song room”: a private karaoke room your group rents by the hour, or a coin booth paid by the song. Look up a few song numbers before you go, confirm the time at the counter and sing without worrying about the score.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does noraebang mean?

Noraebang (노래방) combines norae, “song”, and bang, “room”. It means a karaoke venue where your group sings in its own private room rather than on a public stage. A coin noraebang is a cheaper version with small booths paid by the song.

Are there English songs at Korean noraebang?

Yes. The big TJ and KY catalogues include thousands of English songs in a separate “POP” section of the songbook, and newer tablets let you search by English title. Very recent releases may be missing, so have a few backups ready.

How much does noraebang cost in Korea?

Prices vary by area and time. As a rough guide in 2026, a regular room is often around 15,000–30,000 won an hour, and at many coin noraebang 1,000 won buys a few songs. Check the price at the counter or the machine before you start.
