Public Restrooms in Korea: What to Know

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 9 November 2026

How to find and use public restrooms in Korea, from subway stations and markets to malls, with useful Korean words and practical tips.

When you need a restroom in Korea, look for the word 화장실 (hwajangsil). You will see it on signs in subway stations, parks, department stores and cultural sites. Public toilets are usually free, but facilities inside a café or restaurant may be intended for customers. In busy areas such as Myeongdong, Seoul, it helps to know where to look before you need to go.

Where to find a restroom

Subway stations are a useful first choice in Seoul and other large cities. At Seoul Station, which serves Lines 1 and 4 as well as the KTX trains, follow the 화장실 signs in the station concourse. Station toilets may be inside the fare gates, and arrangements differ by station. If you are not sure whether you need to tap through a gate, ask a station employee before entering or leaving the paid area.

Large department stores and shopping centres are another good option. In Seoul, try Lotte Department Store near Euljiro 1-ga Station on Line 2, or COEX Mall by Samseong Station on Line 2. Follow the signs rather than assuming the restroom is on the nearest floor. At traditional markets, including Gwangjang Market near Jongno 5-ga Station, look for a marked public facility; the food alleys selling bindaetteok (mung-bean pancakes) can be crowded, so it is worth locating the restroom before sitting down to eat.

Use a map app and these Korean words

Search 화장실 in Naver Map or KakaoMap to find nearby facilities. Results can include public toilets and restrooms inside commercial buildings, so check the map listing and signs when you arrive. Seoul Metro’s official website also has station information if you are planning around a particular station. For details that may change, check current information in the map app or on the official site.

These words can help you read signs or ask for directions:

  • 화장실 (hwajangsil): restroom or toilet
  • 남자 화장실 (namja hwajangsil): men’s restroom
  • 여자 화장실 (yeoja hwajangsil): women’s restroom
  • 장애인 화장실 (jangaein hwajangsil): accessible restroom
  • 화장실 어디예요? (hwajangsil eodiyeyo?): Where is the restroom?

What to expect inside

Facilities vary. Newer subway stations, museums and shopping centres often have Western-style toilets, while older buildings and some smaller public facilities may have a squat toilet. If you prefer a Western-style toilet, a large mall such as COEX or a major railway station is a sensible place to try. Restrooms can also have electronic bidet seats. The controls may include Korean labels, so look for a clearly marked stop or flush button and avoid pressing unfamiliar buttons at random.

Some toilets provide paper, but it is wise to carry a small packet of tissues. Follow the signs in the stall: if a bin is provided with instructions not to flush paper, use it. Never flush wet wipes, sanitary products or other items that do not break down easily. In a busy public toilet, leave the stall reasonably tidy for the next person.

Restrooms at attractions and parks

At major sights, use the facilities marked on site rather than leaving the area and hoping to find another one nearby. Gyeongbokgung Palace is beside Gyeongbokgung Station on Line 3, and its grounds have visitor facilities; look for the restroom signs as you explore. At large outdoor places such as Yeouido Hangang Park, toilets are spread across a wide area, so check a map or park sign near your entrance before walking far along the river.

In popular neighbourhoods, a café is not automatically a public restroom. Staff may reserve facilities for customers, and some smaller businesses keep a door code or key at the counter. If you plan to stop for a drink, ordering a coffee or tea is a polite way to use the customer facilities. In Hongdae, near Hongik University Station on Line 2, a large café or shopping complex may be easier to navigate than a small bar on a crowded side street.

Accessibility and a little planning

Look for 장애인 화장실 on signs when you need an accessible facility. Availability and layout vary, even between nearby stations, so check the station or building information before making a long trip if step-free access is important. At a station, staff can point you toward the nearest restroom and explain whether it is before or after the ticket gates.

Before a long subway ride, a palace visit or a walk along the Han River, use a restroom at a station or large public building while one is easy to reach. Keep tissues and hand sanitizer in your day bag, and remember the word 화장실. Those small steps make it much easier to handle a restroom stop in Seoul, Busan or a smaller city.

Bottom line: A little preparation can make your time in Korea much easier. Keep this guide handy and enjoy exploring at your own pace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where can I usually find a public restroom in Korea?

Look for restrooms in places such as subway stations, train stations, shopping centers, parks, and larger public buildings. Follow restroom symbols or look for the Korean word 화장실 (hwajangsil). Availability and access can vary, so in a smaller area you may need to ask staff at a nearby business.

Are the toilets usually seated or squat-style?

You may encounter both seated toilets and squat toilets, though the type can vary from place to place. Check the stall before entering if you have a preference. Restroom signs and layouts can also differ, so take a moment to look around when you arrive.

Should I bring anything with me to a public restroom?

It can be useful to carry a small pack of tissues, since supplies may differ between restrooms. Check for instructions about disposing of toilet paper, and follow any signs in the stall. If you are unsure where something belongs, ask staff when possible.
