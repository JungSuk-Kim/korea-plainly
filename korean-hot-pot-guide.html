Korean Hot Pot Guide: Jeongol, Budae-jjigae and Shabu-shabu for Visitors

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 14 November 2026

Learn how Korean jeongol, spicy budae-jjigae and shabu-shabu differ, what to order, how to eat them and where to try them in Seoul and beyond.

Photo: Jo del Corro, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

A Korean hot pot is usually cooked at your table and shared. The pot may arrive bubbling, or the staff may bring broth and ingredients for you to cook. Three names to know are jeongol (전골), budae-jjigae (부대찌개) and shabu-shabu (샤브샤브). They are not interchangeable: each has a different broth, cooking style and set of add-ons.

Jeongol: a mixed, savoury pot

Jeongol is a broad category of hot pot. Restaurants arrange ingredients such as beef, seafood, mushrooms, tofu or dumplings in a shallow pot, then add broth and cook it at the table. The exact ingredients depend on the name of the dish: haemul-jeongol (해물전골) is seafood hot pot, while mandu-jeongol (만두전골) features dumplings.

Expect a fuller meal than a clear-broth soup. The broth may be mild, spicy or rich, so ask before ordering if you avoid chilli: “Maewo-yo?” (매워요?) means “Is it spicy?” Search Naver Map for the specific type, such as “만두전골,” to find nearby options.

Budae-jjigae: spicy sausage and kimchi stew

Budae-jjigae is a spicy stew made with ingredients such as kimchi, sausage, ham, tofu, instant noodles and baked beans. Its history is linked to the postwar period and the use of processed foods around US military bases. The most famous place to try it is Uijeongbu Budae-jjigae Street, a short walk from Uijeongbu Station on Subway Line 1. Odeng Sikdang is one of the street’s well-known restaurants.

It usually arrives as a bubbling pot, and the staff may cook it for you or tell you when it is ready. The noodles soak up broth quickly, so eat them while they are still springy. You can often add extra ramyeon (라면사리), cheese or sausage. If you want less heat, ask “Deol maepge hae juseyo” (덜 맵게 해 주세요), meaning “Please make it less spicy”; the kitchen may not be able to change a pre-made base much. A serving commonly costs around ₩10,000–₩16,000 per person (2026), with extras charged separately.

Shabu-shabu: cook thin slices in broth

Korean shabu-shabu generally comes with a pot of broth, thinly sliced beef, vegetables and dipping sauces. Swish a slice of meat through the simmering broth until cooked, then dip it in the sauce. Add vegetables in batches rather than crowding the pot. Some restaurants offer a set with mushrooms or seafood, so check what is included before ordering.

The meal often continues after the meat and vegetables: ask whether kalguksu (칼국수), hand-cut noodles, or a final fried rice or rice porridge course is available. In Seoul, restaurants around Hongdae and Hapjeong stations on Line 2 and Line 6 offer many casual group-meal options; use “샤브샤브” in Naver Map to locate a branch near you. A typical meal may cost around ₩15,000–₩30,000 per person (2026), depending on the meat and set.

How to order and eat without guesswork

  • Check the serving size. Many hot pots are priced per person and require a minimum order of two servings. Ask “I-inbun dwaeyo?” (2인분 돼요?)—“Can we order two servings?”—if your group is small.
  • Look for add-ons. “Sari” (사리) means an extra ingredient, often noodles. “Ramyeon-sari” is an extra packet of noodles; “bap” (밥) is rice. Ask whether an add-on is included or costs extra.
  • Wait for the broth to boil. For budae-jjigae and jeongol, let the pot simmer until the ingredients are hot. At shabu-shabu, cook meat and vegetables in the broth yourself unless staff offer to help.
  • Share safely. Use the serving spoon to move food from the pot to your bowl. If you have a food allergy, show the ingredient written in Korean where possible; broths and sauces can contain seafood, soy or sesame.

Picking the right pot

Choose jeongol for a substantial pot whose ingredients depend on the restaurant’s specialty; budae-jjigae for a spicy, salty meal with sausage, ham and noodles; and shabu-shabu for a more hands-on meal with thin meat cooked in broth. For a mixed group, look at the menu photos and confirm the spice level and minimum servings before sitting down. Naver Map is useful for Korean-language menu searches and current restaurant details. If your group would rather grill than simmer, see how to eat Korean BBQ, and for more dishes, our first-timer food list.

Bottom line: Choose budae-jjigae for a spicy sausage-and-noodle stew, jeongol for a varied shared pot, or shabu-shabu for cooking thin meat at the table. Before ordering, confirm the minimum servings, spice level and whether noodles or rice cost extra.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between jeongol, budae-jjigae, and shabu-shabu?

Jeongol is generally a communal, simmered dish with a mix of ingredients. Budae-jjigae is a spicy stew known for processed meats and other add-ins. Shabu-shabu features thinly sliced ingredients cooked briefly in hot broth. Recipes vary, so ask the restaurant what is included if you have dietary needs.

Do I cook the food myself, or does it arrive ready to eat?

Often, jeongol and budae-jjigae are served in a pot for diners to share, while shabu-shabu is cooked at the table as you eat. Restaurant setups differ. Follow the staff’s instructions, and ask for help if you are unsure when an ingredient is ready.

Can I ask for a less spicy or meat-free option?

Some versions are spicy, especially budae-jjigae, and broth or sauces may contain ingredients you avoid. Tell staff clearly about allergies or dietary restrictions before ordering, and ask whether a milder option or ingredient changes are possible. Do not assume a dish is vegetarian just because it contains vegetables: broths are often made with beef, anchovy or seafood. Our vegetarian, vegan and halal food guide has phrases that help.
