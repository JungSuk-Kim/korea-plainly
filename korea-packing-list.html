Korea Packing List: Summer, Winter and Every Season (Interactive Checklist)

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 15 October 2026 · Updated 28 October 2026

Korea packing list for summer heat and monsoon rain, freezing winters and changeable spring and autumn, with a checklist you can tick off and print.

Photo: Jnpet, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

What you pack for Korea depends mostly on the season. Summers are hot, humid and wet, winters are properly cold and dry, and spring and autumn can feel like two seasons in one day. A few things matter whatever the month, from the right plug adapter to socks you are happy to show off when you take your shoes off. Pick your season in the checklist below and tick things off as you pack: your ticks are saved on this device, and you can print the list. Below it you will find what to bring for a Korean summer and winter in more detail, and what you can safely leave at home.

Your checklist

Korea packing list for summer (June to August)

A Korean summer has two halves. The rainy season (jangma) usually arrives in late June and lasts about a month, with days of heavy, warm rain. After it, late July and August are the hottest weeks: Seoul often reaches the low 30s °C, and the humidity makes it feel hotter. Typhoons are most likely from August into September and mostly affect Jeju and the south coast.

  • Light, loose clothes that dry quickly. Linen and thin synthetics beat heavy cotton. You will sweat on every walk between subway exits.
  • A compact umbrella. It doubles as a sunshade, which is common in Korea. If you forget one, convenience stores sell cheap umbrellas the moment it starts to rain.
  • Shoes that cope with puddles. Sandals with straps or quick-drying trainers are better than canvas shoes that stay wet all day.
  • A thin layer for indoors. Subways, cafés, cinemas and buses are often strongly air-conditioned, so a light shirt or cardigan helps.
  • Sun cream, a hat and insect repellent. All are easy to buy in Korea too: our Olive Young guide covers Korean sun creams, which many visitors take home.
  • A small towel or handkerchief. Locals carry one for sweat, and many toilets have hand dryers but no paper towels.
  • Swimwear, if you plan on beaches in Busan or Gangneung, or a water park. Some indoor pools and water parks ask for a swim cap; check before you go. Bathhouses are different: the bathing areas are used without swimwear (see our bathhouse guide).

A small handheld fan is one of the most common sights on a Korean summer street. You do not need to pack one; they are sold cheaply everywhere.

Korea packing list for winter (December to February)

Winter in Seoul is colder than many visitors expect. Daytime highs in January are often around freezing, nights drop well below it, and a cold wind from the north can make it feel far colder for a few days at a time. It is usually dry and sunny rather than snowy. Busan is milder, and Jeju is windy and damp rather than freezing. Our winter travel guide has more on where to go.

  • A warm padded coat, ideally long. Knee-length padded coats are what most Koreans wear, and for good reason.
  • Thermal underwear. A thin base layer under your clothes is better than one bulky jumper.
  • Hat, scarf and gloves. Touchscreen gloves help, because you will check your map app constantly.
  • Shoes with grip. Pavements, palace paths and the steep lanes of Bukchon can be icy in the morning.
  • Layers you can take off. Buildings, subways and homes are well heated, and traditional rooms have heated floors (ondol). You will be peeling off layers every time you go inside.
  • Lip balm and hand cream. The air is very dry, both outside and in heated rooms.

Disposable heat packs are sold in every convenience store, so there is no need to fill your suitcase with them.

Spring and autumn: pack for both ends of the day

Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the easiest seasons, but the gap between a chilly morning and a warm afternoon can be more than ten degrees. Pack T-shirts, a jumper or fleece and a light jacket, and add a warmer coat for early March or November. In spring, yellow dust and fine dust can make the air hazy on some days; locals wear KF94 masks, which you can buy at any pharmacy or convenience store. Autumn is hiking season, so bring comfortable shoes if you want to see the leaves in the mountains.

Things people forget

  • A plug adapter. Korea uses the two round pin plugs (types C and F) at 220 volts. Most phone and laptop chargers work on any voltage; check the label on hair dryers and straighteners.
  • Mobile data. Sort out a SIM, eSIM or pocket Wi-Fi before you fly. See our SIM and eSIM guide.
  • The right map app. Google Maps directions are limited in Korea. See Naver Map vs Google Maps.
  • Good socks. You take your shoes off in many traditional restaurants, guesthouses and temples. Shoes you can slip on and off quickly make this easier.
  • A power bank in your cabin bag. Power banks must not go in checked luggage, and Korean airlines no longer let you use them during the flight. Check your airline's current rules before you fly.
  • A small rubbish bag. Public bins are surprisingly hard to find on Korean streets, so locals often carry their rubbish until they find one.
  • Room in your suitcase. Korean skincare, snacks and clothes are hard to resist. Claim your tax refund: see our tax refund guide.

What to leave at home

  • Full-size toiletries. Shampoo, toothpaste and skincare are cheap and good in Korea, and most hotels provide the basics.
  • A towel for the bathhouse. Bathhouses and jjimjilbang hand out small towels and clothes to wear in shared areas.
  • Lots of cash. Cards work almost everywhere. A little cash for markets and street stalls is enough.
  • 110-volt appliances. Without a converter they will not work on Korea's 220-volt sockets, and may be damaged.

If you take prescription medicine, bring it in its original packaging with a copy of the prescription, and check the rules before you travel if it contains strong painkillers or stimulants.

What you can buy in Korea

Do not worry about forgetting small things. Convenience stores are open all night and sell toiletries, chargers, umbrellas, socks, face masks and heat packs, and pharmacies are easy to find in cities (our guide to pharmacies, hospitals and emergency numbers explains how they work). FAQ

What should I pack for Korea in summer?

Light clothes that dry quickly, a compact umbrella for the rainy season, shoes that cope with puddles, sun cream and a thin layer for strong air-conditioning indoors. Insect repellent helps near rivers and in the countryside.

What plug do I need for Korea?

Korea uses types C and F, the two round pin plugs used in much of Europe, at 220 volts. Bring an adapter; most phone and laptop chargers need nothing else.

Can I buy things I forgot in Korea?

Yes. Convenience stores, pharmacies and big supermarkets sell almost everything a traveller needs, from umbrellas and heat packs to chargers and face masks, and many are open late or all night.
