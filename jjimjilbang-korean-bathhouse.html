Jjimjilbang Meaning and Etiquette: How to Visit a Korean Bathhouse

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 16 November 2026

Learn what a jjimjilbang is, what to expect in the bathing and sauna areas, and how to follow local etiquette at a Korean bathhouse.

A jjimjilbang (찜질방) is a Korean bathhouse with hot baths, showers and heated rooms where visitors can relax in provided clothes. The name refers to the heated-room tradition, but many places combine it with a public bath. For a first visit, the key difference is simple: the bathing area is usually divided by gender and used nude, while shared sauna rooms are mixed-gender and require the facility’s uniform.

These places are part of everyday life, not just a tourist attraction. You might see families, friends or people stopping in after work. At Spa Land in Busan’s Centum City, for example, the bath and sauna experience is part of a large complex connected to Centum City Station on Subway Line 2. Aquafield Goyang, inside Starfield Goyang, is another option near Samsong Station on Line 3. Check current details on Naver Map or the facility’s official site before you go.

What to bring and what to expect at reception

You generally do not need to bring a towel, soap or sauna clothes: many jjimjilbangs provide basic items, though what is included varies. Bring a change of underwear and any personal toiletries you prefer. Some places charge extra for items such as a toothbrush or additional towel. Entry is often around ₩10,000–₩25,000 (2026), depending on the facility, day and length of access; specialty spas can cost more.

At reception, pay and receive a key or wristband, sometimes along with a set of cotton clothes. You may also be given separate shoe and clothing lockers. Follow the number on your key rather than trying to remember it. At a large venue such as Spa Land, signs and staff can help direct you between the changing area, baths and shared rooms.

Bathing etiquette: wash before you soak

Take off your clothes in the gender-separated changing room and leave them in your assigned locker. In the bathing area, people usually bathe nude; swimsuits are not worn in the tubs. Before using a bath, wash thoroughly at the shower stations. This is both practical and considerate, since everyone shares the water.

Keep your voice low, avoid splashing, and do not take photos. Tie up long hair so it stays out of the tubs. If you have a tattoo, do not assume every facility has the same rule: look for posted guidance or ask staff before entering. Some visitors book a body scrub, called a seshin (세신), but it is optional. If you want one, ask where to sign up and what it costs rather than assuming it is included with admission.

Shared sauna rooms and the jjimjilbang uniform

After bathing, dry off before putting on the facility’s shirt and shorts. These are worn in the shared jjimjilbang area, where people of different genders use heated rooms and rest spaces together. Follow room signs for temperature and use, and take a break if you feel dizzy or uncomfortable. Do not bring wet clothes into a heated room, and keep conversations quiet when others are resting.

Many facilities have rooms with different materials or temperatures, but the names and setup vary. At a large spa, ask staff which rooms are suitable for a first visit and whether any require extra payment. A small towel can be folded into a sheep-like shape on your head, a familiar jjimjilbang look, but it is just for fun—not a bathing rule.

Food, rest and useful Korean words

Food is part of the experience at many larger bathhouses. Look for sikhye (식혜), a sweet rice drink often served chilled, and maekbanseok gyeran (맥반석 계란), eggs cooked in a heated sauna style. Some places also have a café or snack counter. Bring your wristband when ordering: charges may be recorded and settled when you leave, though payment systems differ.

Useful words include mokyoktang (목욕탕), a public bath; sauna (사우나), a sauna; and mul (물), water. You may see a sign for the women’s or men’s changing room, so check before entering. If you are unsure where to go, show your locker key and ask an employee. A simple “Eodiro gaseyo?” (어디로 가세요?) means “Where should I go?”

Planning a first visit

Allow time to change, wash and explore rather than treating the visit like a quick shower stop. If you are visiting with someone, agree on a meeting point in the shared area because the bathing rooms are separate. Do not assume every location allows overnight stays or includes every amenity with admission; confirm the rules for that specific venue. For a straightforward outing, Centum City in Busan is convenient for transit and shopping, while Starfield Goyang pairs a bathhouse visit with a large mall near Samsong Station.

Bottom line: Wash before entering a bath, leave swimsuits out of the nude bathing area, and wear the provided uniform in shared sauna rooms. Keep your locker key with you and check a facility’s tattoo and overnight policies before visiting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I visit a jjimjilbang if I don’t speak Korean?

Yes. You can usually manage with gestures and by following signs or other visitors’ lead. At check-in, staff will explain the basics, though instructions may not always be in English. Keep your locker key or wristband with you, and ask staff for help if you are unsure where to go.

Do I have to be naked in the bathing area?

In the gender-separated bathing area, bathing without clothes is customary. The shared heated rooms are different: visitors generally wear the facility’s provided clothes there. If you feel unsure, watch for signs and follow the usual practice in each area. You can also ask staff to clarify where changing is appropriate.

What should I bring to a jjimjilbang?

Many facilities provide basic items such as a towel and clothes for the shared areas, but what is included can vary. Bring any personal toiletries or hair ties you prefer, and check the facility’s information beforehand if you need a particular item. You will usually receive a locker key or wristband to keep with you.
