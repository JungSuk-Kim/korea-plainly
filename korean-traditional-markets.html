Visiting Korean Traditional Markets: What to Know

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 4 November 2026

A practical guide to eating, ordering and finding your way around traditional markets in Seoul and Busan.

Korean traditional markets are places to shop for groceries, kitchen goods and clothes as well as to eat. A food alley may be busy at lunchtime, while another part of the same market sells dried fish or household supplies. Go with an appetite, but leave room to explore: some of the best snacks are served from small stalls with only a few seats.

Markets can be crowded, and menus may be handwritten or displayed only in Korean. Carry a little cash, bring a translation app, and look at what other customers are ordering. These simple habits make a first visit much easier.

Choose a market that suits your day

In central Seoul, Gwangjang Market is a good introduction to market food. It is near Jongno 5-ga Station on Subway Line 1, and its food alleys are known for bindaetteok (빈대떡), a crisp mung-bean pancake, and mayak gimbap (마약김밥), small seaweed rice rolls. The name mayak means “drug” here as a playful reference to how moreish the rolls are; they contain no drugs. Stalls can be shoulder-to-shoulder at busy times, so keep bags close and be ready to share a bench.

For a more neighborhood feel, try Mangwon Market in Mapo-gu, a short walk from Mangwon Station on Line 6. You will find produce and everyday groceries alongside ready-to-eat snacks such as hotteok (호떡), a sweet filled pancake, and dakgangjeong (닭강정), glazed fried chicken. It works well as a casual food stop rather than a single-dish destination.

Tongin Market in Jongno-gu is known for its dosirak café, where participating shops offer small portions that visitors can assemble into a lunch tray using market tokens. The market is near Gyeongbokgung Station on Line 3. Ask whether the dosirak service is operating when you arrive, since it is a specific offering rather than a way to pay at every stall.

What to order at different stalls

At Gwangjang, bindaetteok is often cooked on a large griddle and served with a dipping sauce. Yukhoe (육회), Korean-seasoned raw beef, is another well-known market dish there. Choose cooked food if raw meat is not for you, and do not feel you need to order a full meal at every stop. A pancake, a few gimbap rolls and a drink can make a filling tasting lunch.

Namdaemun Market, close to Hoehyeon Station on Line 4, is a useful stop if you are already shopping in central Seoul. Look for its kalguksu (칼국수) alley for knife-cut noodle soup, or galchi jorim (갈치조림), spicy braised hairtail, in the area known for that dish. These are sit-down meals, so check the menu or point to a neighboring table’s dish before ordering if you are unsure what a name means.

In Busan, Jagalchi Market near Jagalchi Station on Subway Line 1 is the city’s best-known seafood market. Gukje Market, also in the Nampo-dong area, is close by and has a broader mix of shops and food. At seafood counters, clarify whether a quoted amount is for a whole fish, a portion or preparation before agreeing. If you want a cooked meal, say “여기서 먹을게요” (yeogiseo meogeulgeyo), meaning “I’ll eat here.”

Ordering and paying

Keep these phrases handy: “이거 하나 주세요” (igeo hana juseyo) means “One of this, please”; “포장해 주세요” (pojanghae juseyo) means “Please pack it to go”; and “얼마예요?” (eolmayeyo?) means “How much is it?” Pointing is perfectly practical. If you want another serving, “하나 더 주세요” (hana deo juseyo) means “One more, please.”

Payment options differ by stall. Some accept cards, while small vendors may prefer cash, so carry a few small notes. A T-money transit card is for transport and is not a general way to pay for market food. If you are splitting a meal, ask before ordering several dishes; some stalls have limited seating and expect each customer to order. You may see a small bowl of water or utensils on the table—follow the setup used by nearby diners.

Dietary needs and market manners

Vegetarians should ask about broth and sauces, not just the main ingredient. A noodle soup may use anchovy stock, and kimchi can contain fish sauce or salted shrimp. Useful words include “고기” (gogi, meat), “돼지고기” (dwaejigogi, pork), and “새우” (saeu, shrimp). For an allergy, show the Korean name of the ingredient in writing and ask about shared cooking surfaces; a short phrase cannot guarantee that a dish is free from cross-contact.

At busy counters, order, pay, and move to the side while the food is prepared. Put rubbish where the stall indicates, and return trays or utensils if other diners are doing so. Ask before photographing a vendor or their food close up, especially when the stall is crowded.

Plan the visit without overplanning

Market sections can keep different schedules, and some stalls may close on different days. Before traveling across town, check the market’s current details on Naver Map or the official site. Search the Korean market name as well as its English name, since map listings and stall information are often easier to find in Korean. For a relaxed first visit, pick one main dish, add one snack, and allow time to browse rather than trying to cover the whole market.

Bottom line: A little preparation can make your time in Korea much easier. Keep this guide handy and enjoy exploring at your own pace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I bring cash to a traditional market?

Payment options can vary from stall to stall, so it is useful to carry some cash rather than assume every vendor accepts cards. If you are unsure, ask before ordering or buying. For a larger purchase, confirm the total and payment method before you pay.

Is it okay to bargain with vendors?

Bargaining customs differ by market, stall, and item, so do not assume that prices are negotiable. If you want to ask, do so politely and accept the vendor’s answer. For prepared food or clearly priced goods, paying the stated price is usually the simplest approach.

How can I communicate a food allergy?

Some vendors may not speak English, and a dish’s ingredients may not be obvious. Carry a written Korean translation of your allergy and show it before ordering. If the vendor cannot clearly confirm whether the food is safe for you, choose something else.
