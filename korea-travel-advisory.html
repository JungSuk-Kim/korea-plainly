Seoul Travel Advisory: Is South Korea Safe? Alerts, Drills and Protests Explained

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 13 November 2026 · Updated 23 November 2026

Is Seoul safe? What foreign travel advisories warn about, how to read Korean phone alerts, and how drills, protests and typhoons affect a trip.

Photo: Richard Mortel from Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Short answer: Seoul is one of the easiest big cities in Asia to feel safe in. Street crime against visitors is rare, the subway runs until about midnight, and Hongdae or Gangnam at 1 a.m. is busy rather than threatening. What actually upsets a trip to Korea is more ordinary: a typhoon that grounds Jeju flights, a Saturday protest that closes Gwanghwamun, or a phone alert in Korean that makes the whole train carriage buzz at once. Here is what the advisories warn about and what those alerts and sirens mean.

What foreign travel advisories say about Korea

The US State Department puts South Korea at Level 1, "exercise normal precautions", its lowest level. Most other governments rate it the same way, but the small print is worth knowing:

  • North Korea. Missile launches make headlines abroad, but life in Seoul carries on: shops open, the subway runs and nobody changes plans. The one thing that does change is the DMZ. Tours run only with licensed operators and can be cancelled at short notice when tension rises, so don't book one for your last day.
  • Demonstrations. Large rallies are common around Gwanghwamun Square, Seoul Plaza in front of City Hall and Sejong-daero, mostly on Saturday afternoons. They are usually peaceful but close roads and exits. Watch from a distance; foreigners taking part in political protests can run into trouble with immigration rules.
  • Traffic. Korea's road accident rate is high for a rich country. Cars turn right through crossings while the pedestrian light is green, and delivery scooters ride on pavements. Look both ways even on a green man.
  • Nightlife. Meet people from dating apps in a public place, and keep an eye on your drink in the club streets of Itaewon and Hongdae.

Phone alerts: what that loud buzz means

Korean phones receive government emergency texts called jaenan munja (재난문자). A foreign phone on a Korean SIM or eSIM usually gets them too; on roaming it depends on your carrier. They come in three levels:

  • 위급재난 (wigeup jaenan): the loudest, for an air raid or a major earthquake. Rare.
  • 긴급재난 (gingeup jaenan): urgent, for heavy rain, flooding, heat waves or an earthquake nearby.
  • 안전안내 (anjeon annae): safety guidance, and by far the most common. Usually a cold-wave reminder, a fine-dust warning or a missing elderly person.

Since 2024 the two top levels carry a few English keywords (such as "Earthquake" or "Heavy rain"), but the body is still in Korean. The fix is the Emergency Ready App from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety: it pushes the same alerts in English and, since mid-2026, in over 20 languages, and it shows the nearest shelter. For anything else, copy the text into Papago or point its camera at the screen.

Shelters are marked with a 대피소 (daepiso) sign, often at subway station entrances and in underground car parks.

Civil defence drills and sirens

Once a year, usually in August during the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise, Korea holds a nationwide air-raid drill (minbangwi hullyeon, 민방위 훈련). In 2026 it ran on 20 August: sirens at 2 p.m., people asked to step into the nearest shelter or underground space, and an all-clear at 2:20 p.m. Trains and hospitals kept running. If you hear a siren on a weekday afternoon and nobody around you looks worried, it is almost certainly a drill; the Emergency Ready App lists the schedule.

Weather and air: the risks that really change plans

  • Monsoon and typhoons. The rainy season, jangma (장마), runs from late June into July. Typhoons come mainly from August to September and hit Jeju and Busan hardest, cancelling flights and ferries. Keep a spare day either side of a Jeju trip in late summer.
  • Mountain closures. National park trails at Seoraksan, Bukhansan and Hallasan close on heavy rain, snow or strong-wind days. A closed trail stays closed even if Naver Map still shows the route.
  • Heat. In a 폭염 (pongnyeom, heat wave) alert, community centres and some bank branches open as cooling shelters, mudeowi swimteo (무더위쉼터).
  • Cold. A 한파 (hanpa, cold wave) alert means mornings well below minus 10°C. Pavements and station stairs ice over; shoes with grip matter more than a heavier coat.
  • Fine dust. Misemeonji (미세먼지) is worst in spring. When Naver Weather or AirKorea shows 나쁨 (nappeum, bad), buy KF94 masks at any convenience store or Olive Young and swap the Han River walk for a museum.

Crowds and big events

Since the Itaewon crowd crush in October 2022, police manage big crowds tightly. On nights like the Seoul International Fireworks Festival at Yeouido in early October, Christmas Eve in Myeongdong or Halloween in Itaewon, expect one-way walking routes, closed station exits and trains that pass stations without stopping (mujeongcha, 무정차 통과). Use the station one stop away and walk. Before BTS's March 2026 concert, Seoul sent its first public safety alert in English. If a crowd feels too tight, move sideways towards a side street rather than pushing forwards.

Everyday habits that cover most problems

  • Save your hotel's address in Korean, plus the nearest station and exit number.
  • Remember 112 for police, 119 for fire or ambulance, and 1330 for the multilingual tourist line (not for emergencies).
  • Book taxis through Kakao T so the trip is recorded and you can find the car if you leave something behind. Lost items handed to police are listed on lost112.go.kr.
  • Carry a photo of your passport; the pharmacy guide covers minor illness.
  • Check your entry paperwork before you fly: the K-ETA and e-Arrival guide has the details.

Advisory levels and alert systems can change, so check your own government's travel advice page for Korea a week before you go.

Bottom line: Seoul is safe by big-city standards. Install the Emergency Ready App for English alerts, avoid rally areas on Saturdays, keep a spare day around Jeju or the DMZ, and look both ways at every crossing.

FAQ

Is Seoul safe to visit right now?

Yes, for nearly all visitors. Most governments rate South Korea at their lowest warning level. The realistic risks are traffic, summer weather and crowded events, not crime. Check your own government's advice page shortly before you travel.

Should I worry about North Korea during my trip?

Daily life in Seoul does not change when North Korea fires a missile, and Koreans rarely react. The practical effect for visitors is that DMZ tours can be cancelled at short notice, so leave some slack in your plans.

Will I get Korean emergency alerts on a foreign phone?

Usually yes on a Korean SIM or eSIM, sometimes on roaming. The alerts are mostly in Korean, so install the Emergency Ready App, which sends the same alerts in English and shows the nearest shelter.
