Buying Medicine in Korea: Pharmacies for Travellers

By Jungsuk Kim, Korea Plainly · Published 10 November 2026

How to find a pharmacy, explain common symptoms, buy basic medicine and get help with prescriptions while travelling in Korea.

Photo: Bonnielou2013 This photo was taken with Samsung Galaxy Note 4, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

A Korean pharmacy is easy to spot: look for the sign 약국 (yak-guk), often with a green cross. Pharmacists can help with common problems such as a headache, a cold, an upset stomach or allergies. Most medicines are sold through pharmacies, while convenience stores carry only a limited selection. A little preparation helps, especially if you take regular medicine or have allergies.

Finding a pharmacy

Search 약국 in Naver Map or KakaoMap. In Seoul, you will find pharmacies around busy areas such as Myeongdong, Hongdae and the streets near Seoul Station; in Busan, try the blocks around Seomyeon Station on subway lines 1 and 2. A pharmacy’s location does not guarantee that it is open when you arrive. For today’s status, use Naver Map or KakaoMap; the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s E-Gen portal (e-gen.or.kr) also lists pharmacies and emergency services, though much of its information is in Korean.

Some pharmacies close in the evening or on public holidays, and hours vary. If one is shut, search for another nearby rather than assuming every pharmacy follows the same schedule. In a large station or shopping district, look at the streets around the station as well as inside the main shopping areas.

What to say at the counter

Tell the pharmacist what is wrong, when it started and what you have already taken. You can show the medicine’s package or type a short message into a translation app such as Papago. These Korean words can help:

  • 두통 (du-tong): headache
  • 감기 (gam-gi): cold
  • 소화불량 (so-hwa-bul-lyang): indigestion
  • 알레르기 (al-le-reu-gi): allergy
  • 복용법 (bo-gyong-beop): directions for taking medicine
  • 하루 몇 번 먹어요? (ha-ru myeot beon meo-geo-yo?): How many times a day should I take it?

Say clearly if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking other medicines or allergic to a drug. If you do not understand the directions, ask the pharmacist to write down how often to take it. English support varies by pharmacy, even in central Seoul.

Buying common medicines

For everyday problems, ask for 진통제 (jin-tong-je), a pain reliever; 감기약 (gam-gi-yak), cold medicine; 소화제 (so-hwa-je), digestive medicine; or 알레르기약 (al-le-reu-gi-yak), allergy medicine. You may also see 파스 (pa-seu), medicated patches often used for sore muscles. These are categories, not a recommendation for a particular product. Ask the pharmacist to check the active ingredient and explain the dose.

Many Korean medicine packages have instructions mainly in Korean. Keep the box or leaflet so you can check the name and directions later. Do not combine two cold or pain medicines without checking their ingredients: products with different brand names can contain the same active drug. This is especially important if you already take medicine brought from home.

Convenience stores and late-night needs

Some convenience stores sell a small range of approved over-the-counter medicines, such as selected pain, cold and digestive remedies. Stores near Hongik University Station in Seoul or Seomyeon in Busan may be easy to find late at night, but not every branch carries medicine, and the selection is much smaller than a pharmacy’s. Ask the cashier for 약 (yak, medicine) or look for a medicine label near the counter.

Many cities also run public late-night pharmacies (공공심야약국, gong-gong sim-ya yak-guk), which usually stay open until around 1am, including at weekends and on holidays. Search 공공심야약국 on Naver Map or KakaoMap to find the nearest one, and check its listing before you set off. Our convenience store guide covers what else the 24-hour shops sell.

A convenience store is not a substitute for a pharmacist when you need advice, have a serious symptom or take several medicines. If you cannot find an open pharmacy, search 약국 on a map app or use E-Gen. For a medical emergency, call 119 or go to an emergency department; do not wait for a shop to open.

Prescription medicines and regular treatments

For prescription-only medicine, expect to see a Korean doctor and receive a Korean prescription. A pharmacy may not be able to fill a prescription issued overseas. If you need a refill, contact a clinic before you run out. In Seoul, clinics are common around Gangnam Station and Jongno; tell the clinic staff the generic name, strength and dose rather than relying only on a brand name from home.

Bring your medicine in its original packaging, plus a list of its active ingredients and your usual dose. A photo of the prescription can help explain your treatment, but it does not guarantee that a Korean pharmacy can dispense the medicine. If it is a controlled or specialist medicine, check the rules with the relevant Korean authorities before travelling and speak with your doctor about a safe plan.

When a pharmacy is not enough

Ask for a clinic if symptoms are worsening, last longer than expected or need a diagnosis. Useful signs include 내과 (nae-gwa), internal medicine; 이비인후과 (i-bi-in-hu-gwa), ear, nose and throat; and 피부과 (pi-bu-gwa), dermatology. A pharmacist can help with minor symptoms, but is not a replacement for medical care. For breathing difficulty, severe allergic reactions, major injury or sudden severe pain, call 119. Our emergency help guide explains the numbers to call and how to get help in English. Keep your travel insurance details and passport information accessible when seeking care.

Bottom line: Search 약국 on Naver Map or KakaoMap, show the pharmacist your symptoms in writing, and keep the box. Late at night, look for a public late-night pharmacy (공공심야약국); for anything serious, call 119.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I buy common travel medicines at a pharmacy in Korea?

Many pharmacies sell medicines for common problems such as a cold, allergies, or an upset stomach. Tell the pharmacist what symptoms you have and mention any allergies or other medicines you take. If symptoms are severe, unusual, or getting worse, seek medical care rather than relying on an over-the-counter product.

What should I bring if I need medicine while travelling in Korea?

Bring your regular medicines in their original packaging, along with a note of their generic names and doses. A prescription or a note from your doctor can help explain what you take. If you need a replacement, show the pharmacist the packaging or written information instead of relying only on a brand name.

Will a Korean pharmacist be able to help me in English?

Some pharmacists may speak English, but it is helpful to be ready to explain your symptoms simply. Write down when they started, any allergies, and medicines you already take. A translation app can help, but check that you understand the directions, including how often to take the medicine and any warnings.
