A Beginner’s Guide to Korean Street Food

By Korea Plainly · Published 7 October 2026

The six street snacks to try first, where to find them, and how to order and pay at a stall without speaking Korean.

Korea Plainly · 3 min read

Korean street food is cheap, fast and easy to try even if you speak no Korean. Most stalls sell one or two things, the food is on display, and you can point at what you want. This guide covers the snacks you will see most often, how buying works, and a few habits that make it smoother.

On this page

  1. Where to find it
  2. Foods worth trying first
  3. How buying works
  4. Dietary notes

Where to find it

  • Traditional markets such as Gwangjang Market in Seoul or Gukje Market and the BIFF Square area in Busan, where stalls cluster together and locals eat too.
  • Busy shopping streets like Myeongdong, where carts appear from late afternoon. Prices on tourist strips tend to be higher than in neighbourhood markets.
  • Bunsik shops (분식), small sit-down snack restaurants near subway stations and schools that serve the same foods all day, with a menu on the wall.

Foods worth trying first

  • Tteokbokki (떡볶이): chewy rice cakes in a red sauce that is sweet first and spicy after. If you are sensitive to heat, share one portion.
  • Eomuk (어묵): fish cake folded onto a skewer and kept warm in broth. You usually help yourself to a cup of the broth.
  • Hotteok (호떡): a fried pancake filled with brown sugar syrup, sometimes with seeds. The filling is very hot, so bite carefully.
  • Gimbap (김밥): rice and fillings rolled in seaweed and sliced. A good, mild option for a quick meal.
  • Twigim (튀김): deep-fried vegetables, squid or dumplings, often dipped in tteokbokki sauce.
  • Bungeoppang (붕어빵): a fish-shaped waffle filled with red bean paste, mostly sold in the colder months.

How buying works

  • Look for a price board, point at the item and hold up fingers for the number you want.
  • Eomuk is often self-service: take skewers from the pot and tell the vendor how many you ate when you pay.
  • Many stalls now accept cards, but small carts may prefer cash, so keep some small notes and coins. Our cash and cards guide explains what to carry.
  • Eat standing at or next to the stall. Walking along busy streets while eating is common at markets but can be awkward in crowds.
Tip: Keep the cup, skewer or paper and hand it back to the stall or use its bin. Public rubbish bins are rare in Korea, and vendors usually take their own waste. See how Korean recycling works for the rest of your trip.

Dietary notes

Many sauces and broths contain fish, anchovy or meat stock, and labels are usually only in Korean. Vegetarians should be careful with tteokbokki, eomuk broth and fried items. If you have an allergy, show it written in Korean; our guide on ordering Korean food has useful phrases.

Good to know

  • Stalls open and close at the owner's pace. Evenings and weekends are the most reliable times.
  • Portions are small, so trying three or four things is a normal meal.
  • Busy stalls with a queue of locals usually turn food over fastest, so it is fresher.
